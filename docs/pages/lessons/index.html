Nine lessons from training a small decision model

Thirty tracked runs on a 16 GB Mac, four research questions, five published models. Each lesson below comes with the chart or table that supports it.

Lesson 1

The training method is a wash. Choose on cost.

Full fine-tuning, LoRA adapters, and a post-trained starting checkpoint all land at the same in-domain accuracy. Seven runs across four recipes sit inside a 1.3-point range. The only thing that moved a model out of that cluster was changing what it learned from.

Trained-source test accuracy, every full-data run

full fine-tuneadapter, gold labelsadapter, distilledone dot per seed

The shaded band holds every gold-label and half-teacher run. Teacher-only training is the lone recipe outside it, and it is a change of supervision, not of method.

  • The adapter trains 10.6M weights instead of 441M, ships as 43 MB instead of 2.1 GB, and peaks at 4 GB of memory.
  • The full fine-tune is also more overconfident: it needs a temperature of 2.45 to 2.75 to fix its probabilities, against about 2.0 for the adapter.
  • With 4,000 training questions the full fine-tune led by 1.5 to 2 points, so it is the safer pick only when data is scarce (lesson 6).
Lesson 2

Seed luck is larger than any method difference.

Train the same recipe twice with different seeds and new-source accuracy moves by up to 4.6 points. No gap between recipe means is that large. A single-seed result under about 4 points is noise until a second seed says otherwise.

Two seeds of each recipe on the new-source test set

seed 0seed 1same colors as above

  • The first teacher-only run scored 0.671 and looked like the best model of the project. Its second seed scored 0.626.
  • Paired tests on identical questions measure test-set sampling. They say nothing about training randomness, which turned out to be the bigger source of noise.
  • Two seeds of the baseline told us more than eight single-seed hyperparameter variants.
Lesson 3

Out of domain, model size is the ceiling, not the recipe.

The 4B teacher answers new-source questions at 0.745 zero-shot. Every 0.6B student, whatever it was trained on, stops between 0.61 and 0.67. The knowledge exists in the teacher and does not fit in the student.

New-source test accuracy against parameter count

Kev's models use the same data and evaluation suite but their own training code and newer Qwen bases, so treat the comparison as close, not exact. The trend is what matters: nothing we did at 0.6B moved the cluster, and the Kev family climbs steadily with size.

  • Kev's authors reached the same conclusion for their 0.6B model: capacity, not data, is the bottleneck out of domain.
  • If accuracy on questions unlike your training data matters, the fix is a bigger student. No recipe at this size closes the gap.
Lesson 4

Distillation transfers calibration, not knowledge.

Train the student on the teacher's probabilities and its own probabilities come out trustworthy with no correction. Its accuracy stays where the student's size allows. For a model whose whole job is returning probabilities you can act on, that is the more valuable half.

Temperature needed to calibrate each run

A temperature of 1.0 means the raw probabilities are already calibrated. Every distilled run lands at 0.84 to 0.95; every gold-label run needs 1.96 to 2.75 to undo overconfidence.

Lesson 5

The teacher weight is a dial between in-domain and out-of-domain skill.

More weight on the teacher buys a little transfer and costs in-domain accuracy. Half and half gets the calibration for free and gives up nothing. That is the recipe to ship.

Test accuracy by teacher weight α, full data, mean of two seeds

trained sources (decision-v7 test)new sources (transfer-v4 test)

Loss = α · KL(teacher ‖ student) + (1 − α) · cross-entropy on gold labels. The in-domain drop at α = 1 is significant (−5.6 points, p < 0.0001). The out-of-domain rise is not (+1.7 points, p = 0.66).

  • Gold labels are not required: the teacher-only student still reaches 87% of the teacher's out-of-domain accuracy in a model a sixth the size, answering in about 50 ms instead of 330.
  • At 4,000 questions the same dial showed more strongly: teacher only 0.611 on new-source dev against 0.559 for gold only. Full data narrowed it.
Lesson 6

With little data, LoRA is fragile, and the failure has a fingerprint.

At 4,000 questions, 5 of 15 LoRA runs failed. Three failed because of their configuration and two by chance. A failed run is easy to spot: its decision head stops separating options and dev log-loss freezes at exactly the uniform-guess value.

Outcome of every 4,000-question LoRA run

Collapsed: option scores within 0.002 of each other. Stalled: never left the uniform plateau. Reruns with a second seed separated real failures from unlucky ones.

Development log-loss during training

trained normallystalledcollapsed

A healthy run falls below 0.9 by its first evaluation. A failed run sits on the dashed line, 1.478, which is the log-loss of guessing every option equally. Checking for that value automatically costs nothing.

  • Learning rate 2e-4 collapsed on both seeds. Rank 64 with alpha scaled to rank collapsed; with alpha fixed at 32 it trained. Both make each update too large.
  • Of the twelve sane configurations, two failed by chance, about one in six. No LoRA run failed at full data (0 of 7), and full fine-tuning did not fail in any of its three runs at 4,000 or more.
  • Practical rule: learning rate at or below 1e-4, fixed alpha, at least two seeds, and reject any run whose dev log-loss equals the uniform value.
  • With 1,000 questions neither method left the plateau in two epochs, about 185 steps. That is below the floor for this recipe.
Lesson 7

Thinking is the wrong tool for a decision model.

Letting Qwen reason before answering adds three points that do not reach significance, at 95 times the latency. A decision model's value is answering in milliseconds, and thinking gives that up.

  • As a starting checkpoint for the student, the post-trained, thinking-capable Qwen3-0.6B ended up identical to the base model: 0.810 vs 0.810 in domain, 0.615 vs 0.613 out of domain. It only learned faster in the first epoch.
Lesson 8

Score questions are everyone's weak spot.

Every model picks the right option about 85% of the time and the right yes/no answer about 87% of the time, then scores around 50% when asked for the exact level on a 3-to-5-point scale. No recipe touched that gap.

Seed 0 of each model. Score questions are sentiment and star-rating scales (SST-5, Yelp, Amazon) where the levels are ordered, yet the model treats them as unrelated options.

  • The teacher is weak here too (its own Score accuracy on development data was 0.47 to 0.51), so distillation cannot help.
  • An ordinal loss that rewards being one level off is the obvious next experiment. We did not run it.
Lesson 9

On a 16 GB laptop, memory bugs decide feasibility, not hyperparameters.

Three fixes made the whole project possible, and none of them would appear in a results table.

ProblemSymptomFixEffect
AdamW optimizer state for 441M weights10.1 GB peak, Mac at 19–24% free memory, guard pausing every stepAdafactor for the backbone10.1 → 7.5 GB
Loss read before gradients were computed9.2 GB peak for a 10.6M-parameter adapterEvaluate loss and gradients together so gradient checkpointing holds9.2 → 4.0 GB
Casting the fp32 model to bf16 for publishing18% of a smoke model's decisions flippedEvaluate and publish in the training precision0 flipped
Two GPU jobs at onceSwap grew 3.6 GB; the guard stopped a 2.6-hour labeling runEvery job runs alone, in one queue, at low priorityno further aborts
Queue waited on its own shellIdle for 5 minutes after labeling finishedWait on a process ID, not a command-line match5 min lost
  • The memory guard, which pauses at 25% free and aborts at 12%, never had to stop a training run once jobs ran one at a time.
  • Every run, including the stopped ones, is on GitHub with its config, provenance and logs, so none of this had to be reconstructed from memory.
